Half a century of built-up land in Toruń and Bydgoszcz reconstructed from the Landsat satellite archive

Using satellite images from the last fifty years, we reconstructed how built-up land grew in Toruń and Bydgoszcz and how the cities spread into the surrounding fields. A free app lets you do the same for any town, without creating an account.

About the projectGrzegorz Górniak, Mieczysław Kunz · Department of Geoinformation and Environmental Remote Sensing, Faculty of Earth Sciences and Spatial Management, Nicolaus Copernicus University in Toruń

The Landsat archive spans more than fifty years of observations (Crawford et al., 2023), whereas present-day 10 m land cover maps such as Dynamic World exist only since 2015 (Brown et al., 2022). The project links these two sources. Built-up labels from the 2025 map train a Random Forest classifier (Breiman, 2001), which classifies Landsat composites from 1985–2025 without retraining. An extent constraint and an irreversibility rule for built-up land turn the result into a consistent series of 30 m maps (series A). The 1985 map then trains a model for the Landsat MSS data of 1975 and 1980.

With this series, the project tests how much of the agreement of historical maps with independent data comes from the classifier itself and how much from the constraints, compares the series with a static model (the present-day extent assigned to every epoch) and repeats the procedure in a second city. It then describes the sprawl of both cities: the growth of built-up area, its relation to population (SDG indicator 11.3.1), patch configuration, concentration and directions of expansion.

How it worksfrom one present-day map to fifty years of built-up land

Teacher: Dynamic World 2025

The 10 m annual label is the mode of the labels of Sentinel-2 scenes from June–September 2025 (Brown et al., 2022). From it we draw 1500 built-up points and 1500 points of other classes (seed 42).

Student: Random Forest on Landsat 30 m

19 features: six bands of the summer composite, six indices (NDVI, NDBI, NDWI, MNDWI, BU, BSI), two GLCM texture measures (contrast and entropy of the NIR band), four spring indices and the NDVI amplitude (Haralick et al., 1973; Tucker, 1979; Zha et al., 2003; Xu, 2006). 300 trees, 4 features per split.

Travelling back in time: 1985–2025

The same model classifies median composites from a three-year window around each epoch; TM and ETM+ images are harmonised to the OLI scale (Roy et al., 2016). The threshold p(built-up) ≥ 0.55 was chosen on CORINE 2006 cores (0.65 in the app, chosen for different input data; see the App section).

Order in the series: constraints

Series A is Bt = M ∩ ⋃s≤t Rs: built-up land can appear only within the extent mask M = DW 2015 ∪ DW 2025, and once detected it remains built-up.

Second student: MSS 1975 and 1980

The 1985 series A and pseudo-labels of old built-up cores train a model on 60 m MSS data, normalised to 1985 on pseudo-invariant features (Schott et al., 1988; Canty et al., 2004).

Evaluation and urban analyses

Validation on shared point panels with confidence intervals (Olofsson et al., 2014; Stehman, 2014), a test in a second city without recalibration, then sprawl indicators: SDG 11.3.1, patches, Gini, Clark gradient, directions (Clark, 1951; UN-Habitat, 2025).

Glossarykey terms used on this page and in the app

Landsata series of American satellites that have imaged the whole Earth every 16 days since 1972; the images are free.
30 m pixelthe smallest square of a Landsat image and of our map, 30 × 30 m, roughly a plot with a house and garden.
Built-up landbuildings, roads, car parks and other sealed surfaces visible from a satellite.
Epochthe year for which a map is made (1975, 1980, … 2025); the map combines images from that year and the two neighbouring years.
Dynamic Worlda present-day, very detailed (10 m) land cover map from the Sentinel-2 satellites, available since 2015; for us a labelled ‘textbook’.
Random Foresta program that learns from examples: hundreds of simple ‘decision trees’ vote on whether a pixel is built-up.
Probability and thresholdthe share of trees voting ‘built-up’; a pixel is classed as built-up when it exceeds the threshold (0.55 in the project, 0.65 in the app).
Series Aour map series, in which built-up land once detected stays built-up and can appear only where it exists today.
Suburban ringa belt of land around the city boundary: 0–2.5 km and 2.5–5 km.
F1 (agreement)a measure of how well our map overlaps a reference map; 100% is full agreement.
Patch (cluster)a group of adjoining built-up pixels; fewer patches for the same area means more compact built-up land.
Growth formsinfill (filling gaps), edge expansion and leapfrog (a new estate in open fields).

Land Cover Rewind appyour area, no Google account · folder aplikacja/ in the repository · Zenodo: 10.5281/zenodo.24332551 · Python · Streamlit · Microsoft Planetary Computer

The project notebook runs in Google Earth Engine and needs a Google account. The Land Cover Rewind app runs an analogous procedure (with the differences described below) on open data from the Microsoft Planetary Computer STAC catalogue (Microsoft Open Source et al., 2022), which can be read anonymously: Landsat Collection 2 Level 2 (TM, ETM+, OLI) (Crawford et al., 2023) and the Impact Observatory annual 10 m land cover maps (Karra et al., 2021), which take the role of Dynamic World: they are the teacher in the local mode and define the extent mask. The Random Forest model runs in scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011) on the user’s computer.

What it does

  1. The user draws an area on the map or loads a GPKG, GeoJSON or SHP file.
  2. The app searches Landsat scenes in a three-year window around each epoch 1985–2025, masks clouds (QA_PIXEL), harmonises TM and ETM+ to OLI (Roy et al., 2016) and computes 19 features (the model uses 18; the NDVI amplitude is left out).
  3. The Random Forest model (300 trees, 4 features per split) gives the built-up probability and a four-class map.
  4. The extent mask M (30 m pixels with at least 5 of 9 built-up 10 m pixels in IO LULC 2017 or 2023) and accumulation produce a monotonic series (threshold p ≥ 0.65); maps, GIF animations, landscape metrics and growth forms follow.

The pretrained model rf_torun_bydgoszcz_2025 was trained on the project’s 2025 maps (Toruń and Bydgoszcz) and on features computed by the app code. The four-class model transferred from one city to the other kept an accuracy of Toruń → Bydgoszcz: 87.7%, Bydgoszcz → Toruń: 88.4%. The local model is trained in the user’s area on IO LULC 2024 labels.

How to run it (Windows, no programming)

  1. Install Python 3.12 from python.org; in the installer, tick “Add python.exe to PATH”.
  2. Download and extract the app: ⬇ Download from Zenodo (ZIP) or the whole GitHub repository (ZIP, folder aplikacja).
  3. In the aplikacja folder, double-click run_windows.bat. The first start installs the packages (about 5–10 minutes); the black window must stay open.
  4. The app opens in the browser. The on-screen guide leads through four steps: select the area on the map (with a place search), check the settings (each has an explanation under the ‘?’ sign), run the calculations (the progress bar shows the year being computed) and download the results.

A small area (a few km²) with two years usually takes about a minute; an area of about 150 km² with all years takes from several to over a dozen minutes, depending on the connection. Results: GeoTIFF maps (open them in the free QGIS), CSV tables (Excel), GIF animations and a chart, packed into results.zip. The app interface is in Polish and English (switch at the top of the side panel).

Population and SDG indicator 11.3.1built-up land from the series and population from the GUS 1 km grid (censuses NSP 2011 and 2021) · new app function since version 1.3.0

The UN indicator SDG 11.3.1 is the ratio of the land consumption rate (LCR) to the population growth rate (PGR), abbreviated LCRPGR (UN-Habitat, 2025). A value of 1 means that built-up land and population grow at the same rate; above 1 built-up land grows faster than the number of inhabitants, and a negative value appears when built-up land grows while population falls. Population comes from the 1 km grids of the 2011 and 2021 national censuses (Główny Urząd Statystyczny, 2022; Główny Urząd Statystyczny, 2024), and built-up land at the census dates is interpolated linearly between the 2010, 2015 and 2020 epochs.

How to compute it for your own area

After computing the series, open the panel “SDG indicator 11.3.1” in the app and load a population grid of two dates: two GUS files (column tot, identifier code) or one file with two columns. The app reports population, built-up area and built-up land per capita for the whole area, LCR, PGR and LCRPGR, and the indicator class for every cell lying wholly inside the area. With stable population (|PGR| below 0.13% per year) the ratio is flagged as not interpretable. On the command line the option --population does the same.

Within the city boundaries, the app (pretrained model) gave LCRPGR of −2.26 in Toruń and −0.76 in Bydgoszcz. The values differ from series A because the app uses other data (Microsoft Planetary Computer, IO LULC mask) and another threshold; the sign and the rate of population decline are the same.

Fig. 1

Two-stage label transfer scheme

Scheme of the whole procedure. In the first stage, the Dynamic World 2025 map is the teacher of a Random Forest classifier trained on 30 m Landsat features; the classifier produces raw maps of the epochs 1985–2025, which after the extent constraint (extent mask M) and accumulation give series A. Alongside, a static model is built: the present-day built-up extent assigned to every epoch. In the second stage, the 1985 series A and pseudo-labels train a classifier on 60 m MSS data, which reconstructs 1975 and 1980. The bottom of the scheme marks the validation on shared point panels and in the second city.

The scheme was drawn in matplotlib from the model cards of the notebook (number of features, training sample, threshold). Stages: training on the Dynamic World 2025 label (1500 built-up points and 1500 points of other classes, in Toruń only, seed 42), threshold 0.55 chosen on CORINE 2006 cores, constraints M = D2015 ∪ D2025 and accumulation of detections in epoch order. Sources of methods and data: Breiman (2001), Gorelick et al. (2017), Brown et al. (2022).

The figure separates two sources of information in a historical map: the classifier, which recognises built-up land in old images, and the constraints, which impose where built-up land can appear and that a pixel once built-up stays built-up. The project measures the contribution of each separately.

Location of the study area

(a) The Kuyavian-Pomeranian Voivodeship within Poland and the neighbouring countries; (b) Toruń and Bydgoszcz within their administrative boundaries in the voivodeship, with the Vistula.

Map from Natural Earth data (country and voivodeship boundaries, rivers) and the GUGiK State Register of Boundaries, in PL-1992 (EPSG:2180). The analysis zones (the city and the rings 0–2.5 km and 2.5–5 km from the boundary) cover 466.2 km² around Toruń and 635.1 km² around Bydgoszcz.

Both cities lie in the Toruń–Eberswalde ice-marginal valley, 45 km apart. Bydgoszcz is therefore a good test of transferring the model from Toruń without retraining, but it does not settle transferability to cities with a different climate and built form.

Fig. 3

Agreement of the map versions with the reference data and with the static model

(a) Toruń, (b) Bydgoszcz: F1 of the built-up class with 95% confidence intervals for four map versions (raw RF-S, masked RF-M, accumulated RF-K and series A RF-MK) and for the static model, against six independent sources (WSF Evolution 1985 and 2010, CORINE cores 1990 and 2000, Urban Atlas 2012 and 2021). (c) F1 difference between the static model and series A; an asterisk marks a confidence interval that excludes zero.

For every test, a shared panel of 1000 points was drawn (500 each in the built-up and non-built strata of the series A map, seed 42). All map versions were assessed on the same points with a stratified estimator; F1 is the harmonic mean of producer’s and user’s accuracy. Confidence intervals come from a stratified bootstrap (500 replicates), and differences were computed pairwise on the same points. Sources of methods and data: Büttner (2014), Olofsson et al. (2014), Stehman (2014), Marconcini et al. (2020).

F1 of series A against independent sources ranged from 62.1% (Toruń, CORINE 1990 cores) to 76.8% (Bydgoszcz, WSF Evolution 2010). The raw maps were weaker than series A in all tests, by 4.6–22.6 percentage points (differences computed from the agreement table). The advantage of the series over the static model was conclusive in four of six tests for 1985–2000 and vanished for the 2010–2020 maps, when the present-day extent alone approximates built-up land well.

In 1985, series A detected 71.0% of persistent built-up land around Toruń and 72.4% around Bydgoszcz, and in 2025 98.5% and 99.0%. Part of the built-up land present in WSF Evolution in 1985 was picked up by the series with a delay of at least 15 years (16.2% and 12.5%). Five-year increments therefore contain some ‘late’ detections, and states are better compared by decade.

Fig. 10

What new built-up land replaced: transition maps and zoom windows

(a, b) Land cover transitions 1990 → 2025 in both study areas; (1–6) 1.5 × 1.5 km windows in the rings with the largest share of infill, edge expansion and leapfrog; (c, d) new built-up land 1990–2025 by 1990 land cover class and zone.

The origin of new built-up land is the class of the 1990 four-class map in pixels that series A counts as built-up only after 1990. New built-up land appeared in 95.8% (Bydgoszcz) and 98.5% (Toruń) on cropland, grassland and bare soil; trees and forest accounted for 3.9% and 1.4%, and 8.0% in the city of Bydgoszcz. In the windows, the dominant form made up 48.9–61.8% of new built-up land. The forest share may be underestimated, because the 1990 maps underestimate forest area.

Neither city leads on all indicators. Bydgoszcz has a larger increase in the median distance of new built-up land, a larger fall in the Gini coefficient and a higher leapfrog share. Barriers and validation

A24

Barriers to built-up growth

Barriers in both cities (a separate colour for each barrier, line style by restriction class) over built-up land of 1985 and new built-up land 1985–2025, and the built-up share in the 500 m ring.

The barriers are forms of nature protection (WFS of the General Directorate for Environmental Protection), military areas and airports. For each barrier, the RF/Landsat built-up share inside it and in a 500 m ring around it was computed in successive epochs; the black dashed line is the whole study area.

Inside nature reserves the built-up share stays close to zero throughout the period, and inside airports it amounts to a few per cent. Around the Toruń military areas, built-up land in the 500 m ring rises from about 33% to about 49%, while inside them it stays at a few per cent (read from the chart). Built-up land therefore accumulates at the edges of barriers, not within them.

A25

Agreement of maps with independent sources

F1 of the built-up class of the RF/Landsat, RF/Sentinel-2 and Dynamic World maps against independent sources.

The F1 score of the built-up class was computed for the RF/Landsat 30 m, RF/Sentinel-2 10 m and Dynamic World 10 m maps against independent sources: CORINE patch cores, Urban Atlas and WSF Evolution (stratified estimator).

The F1 of the RF/Landsat maps lies between about 62% and 77% and is higher in Bydgoszcz than in Toruń for the same sources. Against Urban Atlas 2021 the three compared maps give similar values, about 67–76% (read from the chart). Values from different sources are not directly comparable.
